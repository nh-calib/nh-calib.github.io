Where existing calibration stops

Sensors mounted on one vehicle rarely share a field of view or a modality, and mounts drift after maintenance. Three constraints decide whether a calibration method can run on such a platform at all.

No target, no calibration site

Wheeled-vehicle motion is the reference. Any log containing ordinary turns carries the information the estimator needs, so a mount change costs a drive rather than a booked facility.

No shared field of view

Sensors are related through the chassis they are bolted to, not through a scene they both observe. Front and rear LiDARs, or LiDAR against radar, are handled the same way.

Timing touches one parameter

X, roll, pitch and yaw come from each sensor’s own twist, so they never cross a clock boundary. Relative Y is the single parameter estimated across sensors, and the only one that inherits the alignment error.

Alignment stays a method-neutral preprocessing step: this counts how many parameters depend on it, and is not a claim of robustness to bad synchronization.

Abstract

Multi-sensor fusion requires accurate inter-sensor extrinsic calibration. Calibration-room and motion-based approaches impose different environmental constraints, repeatability, and operational burdens. Wheeled-mobility platforms, however, provide motion dynamics as additional calibration information. We propose NH-Calib (Non-Holonomic Constraint-based Calibration), which applies these dynamics to motion-based calibration and thereby offers greater robustness to temporal-synchronization errors. NH-Calib is applicable to any sensor capable of estimating or measuring its own linear and angular velocities. It independently estimates X, Roll, Pitch, and Yaw for each sensor without temporal synchronization. Under locally planar motion, Roll, Pitch, Yaw, and X are estimated separately for each sensor; excluding Z, which is unobservable, relative Y is the only component requiring inter-sensor association. Relative Y is estimated through a sum-form formulation that differences the longitudinal motion integrated over common turn segments, thereby canceling the common forward-speed component of the mobility platform. We experimentally validate the proposed method on the public A2D2, RadarScenes, and Ford Multi-AV datasets.

Method

Every channel enters through one interface — a metric twist in its own frame — and the estimator then splits the six mounting parameters by what each one actually needs. Four fall out of a single sensor’s own motion, Z is not excited by driving at all, and only relative Y is carried across sensors.

1 Sensor ego-motion

The estimator never sees points, returns or raw inertial counts. Each channel is reduced by its own front end to a metric twist: linear and angular velocity, in that sensor’s frame, on that sensor’s clock. LiDAR and radar odometry, GNSS/INS and wheel speed with IMU all produce it, which is why a spinning LiDAR and a 2-D radar can enter the same estimator without a shared field of view or a shared modality.

2 Turn segment extraction

Rotation is the only motion that exposes a mounting offset, so the drive is cut into the intervals that carry it. A segment opens on an angular-rate peak, closes when the rate falls back to the hold level, and is kept only if the heading it accumulates passes the net-rotation level. Straight running is discarded, and every later module consumes these segments rather than raw frames.

3 Roll and pitch

A turn on a locally planar surface has a single rotation axis — the plane normal — and every sensor bolted to the chassis measures that same vector, spread over its own three components according to how it is mounted. Pooling the angular-rate samples of the gated segments gives one representative axis, and the rotation that swings that axis back onto the sensor’s vertical is the mounting roll and pitch. Aligning a single vector can settle only two angles, so yaw is left for the next module.

4 X and yaw

Levelling leaves a planar twist in the sensor’s own frame, and the non-holonomic constraint reads the two in-plane parameters straight off it. The vehicle body has no lateral velocity, so a sensor mounted a lever arm px ahead of the axle must read exactly ω px sideways; under straight running, where ω vanishes, whatever lateral reading survives is the sensor’s own yaw offset ψ. The residual r = sin ψ ṽx + cos ψ ṽy − ω px is driven to zero over the gated segments, with a shared slip coefficient absorbing the speed-dependent departure from the ideal line.

5 Common-mode elimination

Stage 1 closes by expressing every sensor against one reference channel. The absolute numbers carry whatever the vehicle frame and the slip term got wrong, and that error is largely shared: on A2D2 all five sensors miss px by roughly the same −58 mm, so the difference against the reference is clean where the absolute value is not. It is also a diagnostic — where the absolute error is not shared, referencing buys nothing, and that is exactly what Ford shows.

6 Relative Y

Lateral separation is the one thing a single sensor cannot see: it never appears in that sensor’s own twist, only in the difference between two of them. Two sensors on one chassis sweep the same turn about a shared instantaneous centre of rotation at different radii, and that radius difference is Δpy. Integrating each sensor’s forward motion over a common window cancels the unknown vehicle speed and leaves ΔI = Δpy ∫ω dt + βT, one scalar per segment rather than one per frame — which is why alignment error is spent on this parameter alone. Pairwise estimates are then fused by robust weighted least squares.

One real turn, close up: the two ego-motions drawn in time order at their own radii, and the gap opening between them.

And in drive order: the car runs the whole sequence, and every time a gated turn closes it drops one point — that turn’s own path-length difference divided by its own integrated yaw.

Limitations

  • Z is not recovered. Planar driving never excites the motion that would constrain it, which is a property of the trajectory rather than a shortcoming of this estimator.
  • Drives without turning carry little information for relative Y; highway-style logs dominated by straight motion are rejected or estimated weakly.
  • The slip coefficient is carried as a nuisance parameter that absorbs common odometry bias. No physical slip model is claimed from its value.
